Unitranche vs. Senior Secured: What Borrowers Actually Pay for Simplicity

A $4.8 billion covenant-lite unitranche priced at SOFR+575 in March 2026. The all-in math on single-tranche vs. split-stack borrowing, and when the premium is worth paying.

By Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026

Two term sheets lay on the conference table, and the chief financial officer could not tell, at first glance, which one was cheaper. The first offered a senior secured loan from a bank group at a rate that looked like a bargain, plus a second-lien facility from a different lender at a rate that did not. The second offered a single loan from a single lender at a single rate, somewhere between the two. The first deal came with two sets of documents, two lender groups, and an intercreditor agreement that the lawyers said would take six weeks to negotiate. The second could close in three. The CFO’s question was the one every middle-market borrower eventually asks: how much is simplicity worth, and when does it stop being worth it?

The single loan has a name, unitranche, and it has quietly become the default structure for much of middle-market private credit. The question is whether borrowers are paying a fair price for it.

One loan, one rate, one lender — mostly

A unitranche facility blends what used to be two loans, a senior secured tranche and a junior tranche, into a single credit agreement with one interest rate, one set of covenants, and one lender or lender group. The rate is a blend: higher than a standalone senior loan, lower than a senior loan plus a separate mezzanine or second-lien piece bought on its own. The trade is straightforward. The borrower pays more than the cheapest possible senior debt and gets, in return, more leverage, fewer counterparties, and a faster close.

The scale of the structure is no longer a middle-market curiosity. On March 4, 2026, PitchBook reported that WWEX Group’s acquisition by Thoma Bravo would be supported by a unitranche financing exceeding $5 billion from 33 lenders: a $4.815 billion unitranche loan plus a $275 million revolver, priced at SOFR+575 and structured covenant-lite. Ares Capital served as administrative agent. Blackstone was the largest lender, alongside Ares, PSP Investments, Goldman Sachs Asset Management, Oaktree, Blue Owl, and Apollo. Thirty-three lenders on a “single lender” loan tells you how far the structure has traveled from its origins. The unitranche of 2026 is often a syndicate wearing a single-tranche costume.

The structure has been climbing for a decade. Ares Capital led a $1.075 billion unitranche facility for the acquisition of Qlik Technologies by Thoma Bravo, described at the time as the largest unitranche financing by a business development company to date, with Golub Capital, TSSP, and Varagon as joint arrangers. Ares Capital chief executive Kipp deVeer called it a milestone for direct lending as a mature asset class.

“Thirty-three lenders on a ‘single lender’ loan tells you how far the structure has traveled from its origins.”

The price tag, line by line

Start with the senior secured alternative, because that is the rate the premium is measured against. GF Data reported average senior debt pricing of 7.8 percent in the second quarter of 2026, with average total debt of 2.9 times EBITDA on platform buyouts, according to the Association for Corporate Growth’s summary of the GF Data release. That is the bank-market anchor: cheap money, but only for the first two or so turns of leverage.

Unitranche pricing sits above it. Yanne Capital’s August 3, 2026 research paper, drawing on S&P LCD data through June 2026, put median spreads for unitranche facilities sized $5 million to $50 million at SOFR plus 625 basis points, with the 25th percentile clearing at SOFR plus 525 and the 75th percentile at SOFR plus 750. All-in coupons for the tightest quartile of borrowers cleared at 10.2 percent, a 175-basis-point improvement from the first-quarter 2024 peak of 11.9 percent. For growth-stage borrowers with contracted revenue, Yanne found senior secured debt clearing at SOFR plus 550 to 700 basis points, or roughly 10 to 12 percent all-in. The gap between the two structures, at the top of the market, has narrowed to the point where the argument is no longer about the rate. It is about everything around the rate.

The general rule, stated plainly by ClearValue Lending in its 2026 guide, is that unitranche pricing typically runs 200 to 400 basis points above traditional senior bank loans for comparable borrowers, but 200 to 500 basis points below a combined senior-plus-mezzanine stack. The premium over banks reflects higher leverage tolerance: unitranche lenders will go to 4 to 5 times EBITDA where banks stop at 2.5 to 3.5 times. For a borrower who needs more capital than a bank will provide, the unitranche is often the lowest blended cost available.

The worked example: $45,000 a year

The clearest illustration comes from Advisors and Capital’s unitranche guide, which builds the comparison on GF Data’s reported numbers. Take a company with $5 million of EBITDA borrowing 3.0 times EBITDA, close to the 2.9 times average GF Data reported for the second quarter of 2026. The split stack is a $10 million senior loan at the reported 7.8 percent average, costing $780,000 a year, plus a $5 million second-lien loan at an assumed 12 percent, costing $600,000. Combined: $15 million at a 9.2 percent blend, $1,380,000 a year, with two lenders, two agreements, and an intercreditor agreement between them. The unitranche alternative is $15 million at an assumed 9.5 percent, costing $1,425,000 a year. The premium is $45,000 a year, about 0.3 percentage points, for one set of documents, a faster close, and a single lender to deal with. The second-lien and unitranche rates in the example are stated assumptions; the senior rate and the leverage multiple are GF Data’s reported figures.

That $45,000 is the whole argument in miniature. A borrower paying it is buying three things. First, speed: no intercreditor negotiation, which routinely adds weeks to a split-stack close and can kill a competitive auction timetable outright. Second, certainty: one lender’s credit decision instead of two, with no risk that the junior lender walks away after the senior lender has committed. Third, leverage: the extra turn of debt that a bank will not provide at any price. Against those three, the borrower weighs $45,000 a year and a single interest rate that never lets the cheap senior piece show its true cost.

The rate sensitivity cuts both ways. Because almost all private credit loans are floating rate, every one-percentage-point rise in base rates adds $150,000 a year of interest on a $15 million facility, on the guide’s arithmetic. The Federal Reserve’s February 2024 FEDS Note on private credit found mean interest coverage of around 2.0 times for private credit borrowers as of 2023, against about 2.7 times for leveraged loan borrowers. The cushion is thinner in private credit, and a floating-rate unitranche leaves no fixed-rate junior piece to absorb a rate shock. The premium buys simplicity. It does not buy protection from rates.

What the premium buys — and what it no longer buys

Two features of the modern unitranche deserve more attention than the rate. The first is documentation. Yanne Capital, citing Bloomberg DCM data for the second quarter of 2026, found covenant-lite facilities now represent 62 percent of unitranche originations in the sub-$100 million ticket band, up from 34 percent two years earlier. The borrower-friendly document that used to be a large-deal luxury has moved downmarket. That is good news for a borrower who values flexibility and bad news for a lender who will one day need the covenant it gave away.

The second is the identity of the lender. A unitranche with 33 lenders, like the WWEX-Auctane facility, is not a relationship. It is a syndication, and a syndicated unitranche behaves differently in distress than the bilateral one-stop loan the structure was invented to be. One administrative agent speaks for the group, which preserves the single-point-of-contact advantage, but the first-out and last-out economics inside the group are invisible to the borrower and can shape a workout.

When the single tranche is the wrong answer

Here is the counterargument, and it is stronger than unitranche’s champions admit. If a borrower needs only senior leverage, two turns of EBITDA from a bank at 7.8 percent, the unitranche premium is pure waste. The borrower is paying a blended rate for junior capital it never asked for. The right question is not “unitranche or senior secured?” It is “how much leverage do we actually need, and what is the cheapest way to buy exactly that much?” A CFO who answers the second question honestly will sometimes find the split stack cheaper, sometimes the unitranche cheaper, and sometimes find that the bank alone was enough all along.

There is also a discipline argument. A split stack forces two separate credit decisions, and two sets of eyes on a borrower is a genuine control. A single lender writing the whole facility can fall in love with the deal, and the covenant-lite data suggests many have. The Federal Reserve’s note makes the structural point: private credit goes mostly to middle-market firms with $10 million to $1 billion in annual revenue, most of them private-equity sponsored, and the loans are almost all floating rate. A sponsor-backed borrower with a single lender, a covenant-lite document, and thin interest coverage is a borrower whose fate rests entirely on one institution’s judgment. That is a concentration risk the split stack diversifies away.

The break-even test for a CFO

The decision reduces to a piece of arithmetic any CFO can run in an afternoon. Price the split stack honestly, including the junior piece at a market rate, not a hoped-for one. Price the unitranche. The difference, in the worked example, is $45,000 a year on $15 million, or 30 basis points. Then price the things that never appear on a term sheet: the legal fees for a second agreement and an intercreditor negotiation, the weeks of delay in a competitive process, the probability that the junior lender reprices or withdraws, and the value of the extra turn of leverage if the business needs it. If those costs exceed $45,000 a year, take the unitranche. If they do not, take the split stack, and spend the savings on the business.

One more test matters. Ask the unitranche lender who else is in the facility and how the first-out and last-out economics work. If the answer is a single balance sheet, the simplicity is real. If the answer is thirty-three lenders behind an administrative agent, the borrower should negotiate as if it were a syndication, because that is what it is.

Unitranche won the middle market because it solved a real problem: banks would not lend enough, and two lenders were slower than one. The structure will keep winning where leverage, speed, and certainty matter more than 30 basis points. Where they do not, the cheapest loan is still the senior secured one, and no amount of simplicity changes the arithmetic.
