The Covenant Wars: How Private Credit Documentation Became the Real Battleground

Covenant-lite loans hit 90% of private credit deals in 2026. As documentation weakens, the firms with the best covenant data are winning — and the ones without it are flying blind into the next downturn.

By Prime Domain Capital · October 2026

In February 2026, a mid-market software company missed its earnings target by 12%. Under the loan agreement signed in 2021, that miss would have triggered a covenant breach, forced a renegotiation, and given the lender a seat at the table to demand tighter terms. Under the agreement signed in 2025, nothing happened. No breach. No call. No leverage. The covenant that would have caught the problem had been negotiated away eighteen months earlier, in a conference room where three direct lenders were bidding against each other for the deal.

This is the covenant wars, and private credit is losing the documentation battle it spent a decade winning.

The numbers behind the erosion

The deterioration is measurable. Covenant-lite structures — loans with no maintenance covenants requiring periodic financial tests — now account for roughly 90% of new private credit originations, according to Proskauer’s 2026 Private Credit Insights survey. That is up from approximately 75% in 2021 and effectively parity with the broadly syndicated loan market, which the industry once defined itself against.

The EBITDA adjustments tell the same story. Add-backs — the contractual fiction that lets borrowers inflate earnings by adding projected cost savings — averaged 25% of reported EBITDA in 2026 vintage deals, per research from law firm Dechert. In plain terms: a company reporting $100 million in EBITDA may have only $75 million in actual earnings, and the lender agreed to underwrite the larger number.

Leverage multiples have crept upward in parallel. The average debt-to-EBITDA ratio for large-cap direct lending deals reached 5.8x in early 2026, according to Refinitiv LPC data — the highest since 2007. Middle-market deals averaged 4.9x. Both figures use adjusted EBITDA, meaning true leverage against actual cash earnings is higher still.

None of this is secret. Every participant in the market can see the documentation deteriorating. The question is who has the data to price it correctly.

Why documentation data is the new edge

Here is the uncomfortable asymmetry: the largest managers — Ares ($644 billion AUM), Blackstone Credit, Apollo — maintain proprietary databases of covenant terms across thousands of deals. They can see, in granular detail, which protections are being conceded, by whom, and at what spread compensation. A mid-size manager originating 20 deals a year sees only its own book.

This creates a two-tier market. The firms with comprehensive documentation data can identify when a specific covenant concession is being underpriced — say, when the market stops charging for the removal of a capex covenant that historically predicted distress six quarters early. They can also spot the reverse: protections that sound meaningful but have never actually triggered, and can be surrendered cheaply to win deals.

The firms without that data are underwriting on vibes. They know documentation is weakening in general. They cannot quantify how much weaker their specific deal is than the 2021 vintage, or whether the 35 basis points of additional spread compensates for the lost protection.

Yanne Capital’s October 2026 research found private credit pricing 275 basis points inside comparable syndicated bank debt for growth-stage borrowers. Some of that reflects genuine structural advantages — speed, certainty, relationship. Some of it reflects lenders competing away both spread and documentation simultaneously, a double concession that historical data suggests ends badly.

The counterargument: maybe covenants were always theater

The skeptics have a point worth taking seriously. Maintenance covenants, the argument goes, never actually prevented defaults — they merely gave lenders an early warning and a renegotiation right. In practice, most covenant breaches were waived in exchange for fees, and the underlying credit either recovered or it didn’t. The covenant was a tripwire attached to nothing.

There is evidence for this view. A 2024 study by Moody’s found that covenant-lite loans did not default at materially higher rates than covenant-heavy loans in the 2020 downturn, once leverage was controlled for. The protection that mattered was not the quarterly test — it was the initial underwriting: leverage, enterprise value cushion, sponsor quality.

But this argument proves too much. If covenants never mattered, then their removal should not affect pricing at all. Yet the market clearly believes documentation has value — lenders still negotiate over it, just from a weaker position. The honest version of the skeptic’s case is narrower: some covenants were theater, but the blanket removal of all of them, without data on which ones actually predicted distress, is reckless.

What the data actually says about the next downturn

The IMF’s 2026 Global Financial Stability Report warns that payment defaults in private credit are rising from a low base, and that a severe rates or earnings shock could push selective defaults two to three times higher. S&P estimates roughly 20% of private credit exposure sits with software companies whose outlook it calls “highly uncertain” amid AI disruption.

When that stress arrives, the documentation will matter enormously — not because covenants prevent defaults, but because they determine who controls the restructuring. A lender with a springing covenant has a contractual right to force a conversation. A lender without one is an unsecured observer hoping the sponsor acts in good faith.

The firms that will navigate this best are not necessarily the ones with the strongest documentation today. They are the ones with the best data on what their documentation actually says, how it compares to market, and which concessions are likely to matter when the cycle turns. Documentation analytics — systematic, deal-level tracking of covenant terms — is becoming as important as credit analysis itself.

The covenant wars are not really about covenants. They are about information. And in private credit, the information gap is widening.
