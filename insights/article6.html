The Refinancing Wall: What $1 Trillion of Maturing Leveraged Debt Means for Borrowers

About $475 billion of U.S. direct lending and BDC loans mature through 2033, with the heaviest years in 2028-2031. Rating-agency data on when the wall hits, what refinancing costs, and which borrowers face the squeeze first.

By Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026

The loan file on the chief financial officer’s desk was not large. One credit agreement, signed in the spring of 2021, when money was cheap and the lender’s pitch had been simple: one facility, one rate, no bank committee. The balance was a little over $400 million. The maturity date was eighteen months away. The CFO had refinanced loans before, in better markets, and the ritual was familiar. What was unfamiliar was the silence on the other end of the phone. The lender who had chased the deal in 2021 wanted to talk about an extension, not a refinancing. The second lender wanted a higher spread and a tighter covenant package. The third did not return the call at all.

That scene is about to repeat itself thousands of times. Private credit grew up in an era of cheap money, and the loans it wrote in that era are coming due in a market that no longer resembles the one that produced them. The question is how many borrowers can refinance on ordinary terms, how many will pay up for the privilege, and how many will discover that the wall was never really about the calendar at all.

How big is the wall, really

Start with scale, because the headline numbers invite confusion. A Federal Reserve staff note dated August 11, 2026, puts the private credit market and the leveraged loan market at roughly equal size, approximately $1.4 trillion each at the end of 2025, together accounting for around 45 percent of all lending to private non-financial corporations. Private debt funds and business development companies together account for about 90 percent of that market, the Fed found.

Against that base, the maturity schedule looks like this. Crisil Integral IQ, in a note published May 4, 2026, citing Morgan Stanley estimates, counts roughly $475 billion of U.S. middle-market direct lending and BDC maturities through 2033, with about $260 billion falling due between 2026 and 2029 and the most concentrated years running from 2028 to 2031. The wall, on that reading, is a ramp, not a cliff: heavy, but spread across six years, with the worst of it still two to five years out.

The public leveraged markets face their own version. S&P Global figures cited in a 2026 credit-cycle review put speculative-grade refinancing needs at approximately $397 billion in 2026 and another $416 billion in 2027, with roughly $1.2 trillion of leveraged loan and high-yield maturities due from 2027 to 2029. Private credit borrowers compete for the same capital, in the same quarters, as every other leveraged borrower in America.

“The wall is a ramp, not a cliff: heavy, but spread across six years, with the worst of it still two to five years out.”

The rating agencies disagree — and the disagreement is the point

Ask three rating agencies when the maturities bite and you get three answers, because each one is measuring a different slice of the market. Moody’s, in a September 29, 2026 analysis of 32 public BDC portfolios holding about $112 billion of principal, found less than 6 percent of the analyzed principal contractually due in the remaining nine months of 2026, and about 37 percent maturing across 2026 to 2028 combined. The largest single concentration sat in the open-ended 2030-and-beyond bucket. KBRA, in its third-quarter 2026 compendium published November 5, 2026, counted remaining 2026 maturities of $26.9 billion, just 2 percent of its assessed portfolio, and 2027 maturities of $107 billion, or 7 percent. KBRA’s assessed population passed 3,000 borrowers for the first time, with 3,070 middle-market borrowers representing more than $1.4 trillion of direct lending debt in the twelve months to September 30, 2026.

The samples differ, the dates differ, and the definitions differ, which is why the honest answer to “how big is the wall” is a range with a shape attached: modest in 2026, material in 2027, heaviest from 2028 to 2031. A borrower whose loan matures in 2027 faces a different market from one whose loan matures in 2030, and both should plan accordingly.

Moody’s adds the more useful frame. The bigger risk, it argues, is not the maturity schedule itself but what it calls a “resiliency dispersion wall”: a widening gap between borrowers with resilient earnings and supportive sponsors, who should keep straightforward access to capital, and a smaller group combining weak fundamentals with valuation pressure. Moody’s found about $10.4 billion of analyzed exposure, 9 percent of the total, that combines shorter refinancing runways with valuations below 95 percent of cost.

What refinancing actually costs now

For the healthy majority, the wall is a pricing story, and pricing has moved in both directions this year. Median new-issue direct lending spreads fell for three consecutive years, from a peak of 716 basis points in March 2023 to 596 basis points at the end of 2024 and 544 basis points at the end of 2025, according to Priority Income Fund’s July 2026 market review. Entering 2026, spreads at the upper end of the market had fallen to roughly 500 to 525 basis points, with about 85 percent of new-issue LBO spreads pricing below SOFR+550 in the first quarter. Broadly syndicated loan spreads fell further, to 325 to 350 basis points at the start of 2026, from a 2022 peak of 450 to 475.

Then the cycle turned. Lord Abbett, citing PitchBook data in September 2026, put private credit spreads roughly 50 to 100 basis points wider than late 2025, with documentation terms improving for lenders. Deal origination fell sharply: direct lending volume dropped to $33.6 billion across 154 deals in the second quarter of 2026, from $74.7 billion across 217 deals in the first quarter, per PitchBook/LCD data. SOFR stood at 3.65 percent on April 6, 2026, Crisil reported, with average first-lien middle-market spreads still around 500 basis points in late 2025.

The refinancing flow between the public and private markets tells the same two-way story. Roughly $37 billion of broadly syndicated loans refinanced into direct lending in 2025, and roughly $34 billion moved the other way, near parity after years in which the flow ran mostly one direction. That arbitrage is good for strong credits. It is no help at all to the weak ones, who cannot access either market on workable terms.

The squeeze hits some vintages first

The borrowers in trouble share a birth year. Loans originated in the 2020 to 2022 low-rate window often carry coupons of 4 to 5 percent. Refinancing comparable risk today means 8 to 10 percent, and the arithmetic of that gap is brutal for companies whose earnings have not doubled in the meantime. KBRA’s November compendium shows why: median leverage across its assessed borrowers rose to 6.2 times for the first time in more than two years, while median interest coverage held at just 1.6 times. Roughly 20 percent of borrowers had falling revenue. The default monitor reached a record 112 borrowers, about 3.6 percent by count.

The stress is concentrated by sector, not spread evenly. KBRA flags Health Care Services and Technology, with about $36 billion due in 2027, of which $8 billion is owed by companies it assesses at ccc+ or ccc. Octus reported in April 2026 that Sophos, the cybersecurity company backed by Thoma Bravo, faced a March 2027 maturity on its $2.1 billion loan after several private credit firms passed on a proposed $2.5 billion refinancing, leaving the company discussing an amend-and-extend with existing lenders. That is the wall in miniature: a real company, a real date, and a market that declined to compete for the business.

The quiet signals are deteriorating too. Fitch put the U.S. private credit default rate at 6.0 percent in April 2026, a record high, according to Bond Capital’s September 2026 analysis of Fitch data. Lincoln International data cited by Crisil shows 11 percent of loans paid some payment-in-kind interest in 2025, and 58 percent of that was what Lincoln calls “bad PIK”: interest that was not PIK at closing and became so later, because the borrower could not pay in cash.

The case that the wall is a mirage

Now the other side, because the alarmists have been wrong about this market before. Moody’s own conclusion is that the wall appears less severe than some headlines suggest. Less than 6 percent of BDC principal comes due in the rest of 2026. The 2028 to 2031 concentration is years away, and years are exactly what amend-and-extend exists to buy. Most borrowers will refinance.

The capital is also still arriving. Direct lending fundraising reached $129 billion in the first nine months of 2026, up 35 percent year over year, according to Dealroom’s October 29, 2026 report, and Ares Management drew $8 billion at the first close of its latest flagship direct lending fund, targeting $10 billion to $12 billion in equity commitments.

There is truth in both views, and the split is the story. The wall is a mirage for the strong and a cliff for the weak, and the market is sorting borrowers into those two piles faster than it used to. Crisil’s conclusion is the sober one: the wall should surface first as a refinancing and structuring challenge rather than a broad market shutdown, with stress driven largely by sector. That is a polite way of saying the defaults will be selective, the extensions will be expensive, and the averages will hide both.

What borrowers should do before 2028

The practical lesson is about timing, not panic. A borrower with a 2028 maturity that starts the conversation in early 2027 is negotiating from strength. The same borrower starting in late 2027 is negotiating from a calendar. Eighteen months of runway is the minimum for a complicated credit; twelve months is a gamble; six months is a restructuring in all but name. The firms that fare best will map every facility, rank the refinancing options while both markets are open, and price the extension before needing it.

Lenders should be doing the same exercise from the other side of the table. The $10.4 billion of short-runway, sub-95-percent-of-cost exposure Moody’s identified is not a large number against a $1.4 trillion market. It is a large number if it all arrives in the same two quarters, in the same two sectors, while origination volumes are falling and spreads are widening. Dispersion is the risk now, not scale.

The refinancing wall will not arrive as a single event with a date on it. It will arrive as a thousand separate negotiations, some of them cheap, some of them expensive, a few of them impossible. The borrowers who understand which kind they are, and act while they still have a choice, will be the ones who get to keep choosing.
