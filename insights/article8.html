How Long Does a Private Credit Deal Take From Term Sheet to Close?

A clean direct-lending file can close in three weeks. A bank takes three months for the same borrower. The difference is not the paperwork — it is who makes the decision, and which steps run in parallel instead of in sequence.

By Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026

The purchase agreement gave the buyer forty-five days to close. On day thirty-two, the lender’s credit committee sent back twelve conditions, and the borrower’s counsel did the arithmetic out loud: three weeks for a valuation the lender had not yet ordered, two more for an intercreditor agreement nobody had started, and a funding date already nine days past the deadline in the purchase agreement. The deal did not die that afternoon. It died in slow motion, one un-ordered report at a time, while the seller’s patience ran out.

Every private credit deal has a clock, and the clock is the one term nobody negotiates. A clean direct-lending file can move from first call to funded in three weeks. A bank takes two to three months for the same borrower. The difference is not the paperwork. It is who makes the decision, how many people have to agree, and which steps run in parallel instead of in sequence.

The calendar, stage by stage

The fastest way to read a private credit timeline is stage by stage, because the stages are where the days actually go. Global Mortgage Group, a commercial lender that publishes its own five-step workflow, puts it this way: days one to five for the initial conversation, days five to fourteen for the term sheet, weeks two to four for due diligence, weeks three to five for documentation, and weeks four to six for funding. Urgent transactions, on its account, can close in under three weeks. Apati Capital, a private credit lender, gives the same shape in shorter form: term sheets within a week, funding in two to three weeks if the financials check out.

The bank comparison is stark. HotelLoans.net, which tracks both channels, breaks the phases down side by side. A term sheet takes 24 to 48 hours from a private lender and 7 to 14 days from a bank. Underwriting and documentation run 5 to 10 days private against 30 to 45 days at a bank. Third-party report review takes 3 to 10 days private and 15 to 20 days at a bank. Final approval and funding take 2 to 4 days private and 10 to 15 days at a bank. The totals: 15 to 30 days for a private close, 62 to 94 days for a bank. CREquity AI and Gelt Financial give the same bank range in different words, 45 to 90 days from application to close for conventional and SBA loans, with appraisal scheduling and title clearance the two steps most often blamed for the stretch.

ForjeCap, a private lender, publishes a scenario table that shows how much the deal type matters. A clean equipment purchase closes in 3 to 7 business days with a private lender and 20 to 45 days with a bank. A multi-asset restructuring takes 10 to 21 days private and 45 to 90 days or more at a bank. The private process, on ForjeCap’s account, has three stages and two of them run in parallel: intake and collateral review in 1 to 3 days, a decision the same day to two days later, and documentation and funding in 1 to 5 days. The person who reviews the file is the person who approves it, which is the structural reason the private timeline compresses. There is no committee queue to wait in.

The three steps that stall a closing

The first stall is the third-party report. Appraisal, valuation, environmental review, title commitment: none of it can start until somebody orders it, and in most delayed deals nobody orders it until the term sheet is signed. Bank of America’s commercial real estate lending guidance flags appraisal scheduling and title clearance as the two most common drivers of delay, and the same pattern holds in corporate private credit, where the equivalents are the quality-of-earnings report, the valuation opinion, and the lien search. The fix is unglamorous and it works: order the reports the week the application goes in, not the week the term sheet comes back. Running them in parallel with underwriting instead of after it can save a week or more.

The second stall is documentation, and it scales with the number of parties. In a split stack, the intercreditor agreement between the senior and junior lenders is the long pole; the lawyers on the unitranche article in this series put it at six weeks to negotiate. Even in a single-tranche deal, the credit agreement, the security documents, and the conditions precedent take two to three weeks of lawyer time, and every additional lender in the facility adds a review cycle. The $4.815 billion WWEX unitranche that Thoma Bravo’s acquisition required in March 2026 had thirty-three lenders behind Ares Capital as administrative agent. A facility that large does not close fast because the documents are simple. It closes fast because the process is run by people who have done it before.

The third stall is the credit committee, and it is the one borrowers understand least. A file that reaches committee clean gets approved in days. A file that reaches committee with open diligence items gets deferred to the next meeting, and committees meet weekly or biweekly. The three stalls compound: a deferred committee meeting pushes documentation, documentation pushes funding past the purchase agreement deadline, and the deadline triggers the seller’s walk-away right. The purchase agreement’s outside date is the only deadline in the whole process that the borrower cannot extend by asking nicely.

What the fast files have in common

The files that close in three weeks share a pattern, and it starts before the lender is involved. The data room is complete before the first call. The quality-of-earnings report is done, not promised. Management is available for diligence in the second week, not the fourth. There are no surprises in the second week, because the borrower already found them in the first. A lender that discovers a problem on day twenty is a lender that restarts its clock. A borrower that surfaces the problem on day two is a borrower whose clock keeps running.

The lender side matters just as much. Direct lenders underwrite with judgment rather than a fixed scorecard, and the decision-maker is in the room from the first call. Pre-vetted capital sources and table funding arrangements mean the money is available at the moment of closing rather than waiting on a secondary sale. None of this is new. It is the reason private credit exists: the bank’s process is built to be repeatable across thousands of files, and the direct lender’s process is built to be fast on one file at a time.

When speed is the wrong priority

Here is the counterargument, and it deserves a hearing. A lender that closes in ten days without doing diligence is a lender that will be aggressive in a workout, and a borrower that optimizes only for speed pays for it in rate and structure. The Federal Reserve’s February 2024 FEDS Note on private credit found mean interest coverage of around 2.0 times for private credit borrowers, against about 2.7 times for leveraged loan borrowers. The cushion is thin, and a floating-rate loan leaves no fixed-rate piece to absorb a rate shock. A fast close on a thin cushion is a fast close into a fragile capital structure.

There is also a selection argument. The deals that close fastest are the cleanest files, and the cleanest files are the ones any lender would want. A borrower with a complicated file who chooses the fastest lender is often choosing the lender that looked least carefully. The right question is not how fast a lender can close. It is how fast a lender can close without giving up the terms that matter: the rate, the covenants, the flexibility to run the business, and the certainty that the money will actually be there on the funding date.

The test before you sign the term sheet

The practical version of all of this fits on an index card. Order the third-party reports on day one, the same week the application goes in. Ask the lender for its full conditions precedent list before signing the term sheet, not after. Confirm the credit committee date in writing, and find out what happens if the file is deferred. Build a one-week buffer into the purchase agreement’s outside date, because the one deadline that cannot be extended is the seller’s. And price the timeline the way you price the rate: a lender that promises three weeks and delivers six has cost the borrower more than a lender that promised five weeks and delivered five.

The refinancing wall this series has been tracking — about $475 billion of U.S. direct lending and BDC loans maturing through 2033, with the heaviest years in 2028 through 2031 — means more borrowers will be testing these timelines at once. The lenders that close on schedule will be the ones whose process is built for it. The borrowers that close on schedule will be the ones who treated the calendar as a term to negotiate, not a hope to hold.
