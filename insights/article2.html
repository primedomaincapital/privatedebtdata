Direct Lending vs. Syndicated Loans: Where Institutional Capital Is Moving

Private credit now prices 275 bps inside syndicated bank debt. How direct lending overtook the leveraged loan market, and what borrowers should do next.

By Prime Domain Capital · October 2026

On October 7, 2026, a boutique advisory firm published a number that should have been impossible. Yanne Capital’s research found private credit pricing 275 basis points inside comparable syndicated bank debt for growth-stage borrowers — direct lenders winning credit-committee approvals at SOFR plus 475 to 525 for the same borrower profile that syndicated banks were pricing at SOFR plus 750 to 800. A decade of received wisdom, reversed in a single chart.

For ten years, everyone in leveraged finance knew the hierarchy: banks were cheap, direct lenders were expensive, and you paid the premium for speed and flexibility. That hierarchy just inverted. The founders who ran a bank-first process in 2024 and expect the same pricing order in 2027 are anchored to the wrong number.

How the pricing inverted

The mechanism is straightforward, and it has little to do with the cost of capital. Private credit assets crossed $1.7 trillion in the first half of 2026, according to PitchBook — and deployment pressure inside those funds is now the binding constraint on pricing. When you are sitting on hundreds of billions in dry powder with LPs expecting it deployed, you compete. Yanne’s team watched the gap widen through eight consecutive months, not compress.

Banks, meanwhile, are fighting with one hand tied by regulation. Basel III endgame capital treatment on leveraged exposures has tightened bank balance-sheet capacity for exactly the borrower class private credit funds are chasing — a constraint visible in the Federal Reserve’s H.4.1 data. The result is a structural asymmetry: one side of the market is desperate to lend, the other is constrained from doing so.

“The founders who treat the 2027 capital structure decision as a spread negotiation will pay for it in the covenant package.”Alex Ozdemir, Yanne Capital

The warning is precise. When spread stops being the differentiator, structure becomes the battlefield.

Why borrowers moved first

Price is only half the story. Borrowers have been voting with their term sheets for years, and their reasons go beyond basis points.

Certainty first. A direct lender’s committed facility is exactly that — committed. A syndicated process is a roadshow: weeks of marketing, flex language, and the lingering risk that the market moves against you mid-syndication. For a sponsor-backed acquisition on a tight timetable, the value of knowing your financing is done on day one dwarfs a few dozen basis points either way.

Then flexibility. Direct lenders built their franchise on bespoke structures — delayed-draw tranches, PIK toggles, covenant packages negotiated with one counterparty instead of a syndicate of twenty. The IMF’s 2026 Global Financial Stability Report notes that payment-in-kind usage has stabilized for now, though its survey of market professionals suggests they expect it to grow. PIK is the canary: useful in the right hands, a warning sign when it becomes the only way deals clear.

The “one-stop-shop” dynamic compounds the advantage. A single private credit fund can now write the entire capital structure — unitranche, second lien, even preferred equity — where a bank-led deal would require assembling a syndicate across tranches. Fewer counterparties, fewer negotiations, one set of documents.

What lenders should be watching

None of this means the cycle has been repealed. The borrower always shops price; the lender always shops structure — and right now, borrowers are winning both.

S&P Global’s February 2026 report offers genuine comfort: revenues and EBITDA are growing for a large majority of companies with private loans, and cash interest coverage ratios have improved. The corporate earnings picture in 2026 gives no reason to assume broad-based stress. Payment defaults are rising, but from a low base.

The concentrations are where vigilance belongs. S&P estimates that roughly 20% of private credit exposure sits with software companies whose financial outlook it describes as “highly uncertain” — businesses facing direct AI disruption to their revenue models. The IMF adds the structural warning: private credit has never experienced a severe downturn at its current size and scope, and many of the features designed to mitigate risk have not yet been tested. Its models suggest a severe rates or earnings shock could push selective defaults two to three times higher.

Competition is also doing what competition always does to underwriting. When $350 billion in dry powder is chasing deals, terms loosen. Spreads compress. Covenants thin. The pricing inversion is good news for borrowers and a margin story for LPs — but it is also the market telling you that discipline is getting harder to maintain.

Institutional capital has moved, decisively, toward direct lending. The question for the next phase is not whether the shift is real — it is whether the underwriting standards that justified the asset class survive its own success.
