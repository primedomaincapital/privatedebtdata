Private Credit Market Size 2026: How Big Is Direct Lending Really?

Private credit estimates range from $1.5T to $3.5T in 2026. Here is what the data actually says, why measurements disagree, and where growth comes from.

By Prime Domain Capital · October 2026

Somewhere in a midtown conference room this spring, an investment committee stared at two pitch decks. One said the private credit market was $1.5 trillion. The other said $3.5 trillion. Both were presented as fact. Both came from firms that charge six figures for their research.

The uncomfortable truth is that both decks were right — by their own definitions. Private credit has a two-trillion-dollar rounding error, and the industry has learned to live with it.

Three markets wearing one trench coat

The spread in estimates is not a data problem. It is a definition problem, and it breaks down three ways.

First, scope. The Financial Stability Board counts roughly $1.5 to $2 trillion in global private credit assets. Law firm Cleary Gottlieb describes direct lending as now comparable in size to the broadly syndicated loan market — itself around $1.8 trillion. The CFA Institute’s research lands at approximately $2.6 trillion, up from roughly $250 billion in the aftermath of the global financial crisis. CoinLaw’s June 2026 update puts the global figure at $3 trillion, with US direct lending alone near $1 trillion and growth toward $4.5 trillion by 2030. At the widest end, yieldoom’s 2026 benchmark reaches $3.5 trillion. Some tallies count only corporate direct lending. Others fold in mezzanine, distressed debt, specialty finance, and asset-based lending. You are not comparing estimates of the same thing.

Second, timing. Reported assets under management lag fundraising by years, because committed capital deploys slowly. Moody’s 2026 outlook projects AUM exceeding $2 trillion this year and approaching $4 trillion by 2030. Ocorian’s tracker, built on Preqin data, reads $1.38 trillion as of September 2026 — a record, up 2.5% from Preqin’s $1.35 trillion at the end of 2025. The gap between those two numbers is mostly capital that has been raised but not yet lent.

Third, the counter. Manager surveys and regulatory data are built from different universes of funds. For context, the broadly syndicated loan market sits near $1.8 trillion and high-yield bonds near $1.6 trillion. Whichever methodology you prefer, direct lending has grown from a post-crisis niche into a market standing shoulder to shoulder with the public leveraged-finance markets it was once merely an alternative to.

The composition is changing faster than the total

Fixating on the headline number misses the more important shift: what the money is actually financing. Moody’s 2026 outlook flags a clear pivot from traditional corporate direct lending toward asset-based finance — loans backed by pools of assets such as consumer receivables, infrastructure contracts, and equipment rather than a single borrower’s cash flow. Wellington frames the total potential opportunity across every asset class private credit could eventually touch at more than $30 trillion. Private credit is financing data center buildouts, mortgage warehouses, and auto-loan portfolios that regional banks have walked away from.

The manager leaderboard shows how institutionalized the space has become. Ares Management reported approximately $644 billion in assets under management in early 2026. Blackstone’s non-traded BDC, BCRED, stands at roughly $82 billion. Barings runs a $19 billion direct lending strategy. The top 20 managers control an estimated $138.14 billion in dry powder — about 36% of the global total. Industry estimates project fundraising reaching $250 billion annually by 2026, with total dry powder near $350 billion. And the limited-partner base is not cooling: roughly 81% of LPs planned to hold or increase their private credit commitments in 2026.

Why the number still matters

The size debate is not academic. It determines three practical things.

First, deployment pressure. With some $350 billion in dry powder waiting to be invested, managers must put capital to work — and that pressure is already distorting pricing. Research published in October 2026 by Yanne Capital found private credit pricing 275 basis points inside comparable syndicated bank debt for growth-stage borrowers, reversing a decade of received wisdom that direct lenders always charge a premium for speed and flexibility.

Second, the investor base is being rewritten. Wellington estimates US retail allocations to private credit could grow from roughly $100 billion today to $2.4 trillion by 2030, as interval funds and semi-liquid vehicles open the asset class beyond institutions. That flow of capital will change how the market behaves the first time it is genuinely stressed.

Third, you cannot stress-test what you cannot measure. The IMF’s 2026 Global Financial Stability Report acknowledges private credit’s growth while warning that payment defaults are rising from a low base — and that a severe rates or earnings shock could push selective defaults two to three times higher. S&P estimates roughly 20% of private credit exposure sits with software companies whose financial outlook it calls “highly uncertain” amid AI disruption. The industry’s measurement problem is, at bottom, a risk-management problem.

Private credit is no longer a niche allocation. It is a core holding for institutional portfolios and a central pillar of corporate finance. Its exact size will stay contested. Its trajectory is not.
