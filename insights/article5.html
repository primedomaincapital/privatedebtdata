The 6.3% Number: Private Credit’s Default Rate Hits a Record, and the Data Disagrees About Why

Fitch puts the U.S. private credit default rate at 6.3%, the highest on record. Moody’s says 4.0% reported, 5.8% counting payment-in-kind amendments and maturity extensions. The gap between the two numbers is the story.

By Prime Domain Capital · November 2026

In late October 2026, a credit committee at a large direct lender stared at two numbers that could not both be right. Fitch Ratings put the U.S. private credit default rate at 6.3% for the trailing twelve months ended in the third quarter, the highest quarterly reading on record, up from 6.0% in the second quarter and rising every quarter since the third quarter of 2025. Moody’s, looking at the same market over the twelve months to June 2026, found 4.0% of 607 rated issuers in default. The difference was not a rounding error. It was a definition. Count payment-in-kind amendments and maturity extensions among distressed borrowers as defaults, as Moody’s also did, and its rate rose to 5.8%. The market cannot agree on how many of its borrowers are failing, because it cannot agree on what failing means.

That disagreement is now the central fact of private credit, a market of roughly $1.8 trillion that has spent five years telling investors it is the stable alternative to public debt. The numbers underneath the argument are moving in one direction, and it is not the reassuring one.

What the record actually records

Fitch’s 6.3% is a trailing-twelve-month rate, and its path matters as much as its level: up every quarter for a year, from a market that spent the previous decade insisting its defaults would stay low because its loans were senior, secured, and covenanted. The Moody’s analysis explains why the headline understates the stress. Borrowers are using delayed interest payments and maturity extensions to avoid a formal default, and those maneuvers cluster exactly where the stress is. Houlihan Lokey found that default rates among the smallest private credit borrowers, those with less than $100 million of EBITDA, the bulk of the direct lending market, ran at 3.0% on a size-weighted basis and 3.6% by count in the second quarter of 2026, up more than tenfold since 2023.

The public market is pricing the same fear more bluntly. CCC-rated loan spreads hit 2,449 basis points, their highest since May 2009. The stress is visible in the vehicles built to sell private credit to the public. Blackstone’s flagship non-traded business development company, BCRED, posted its first monthly loss in more than three years in February 2026. Redemption requests across non-traded BDCs reached 12.4% of net asset value in the second quarter, a record high, and sponsors fulfilled just 38% of the requests. Semi-liquid vehicles continue to see redemption requests well above typical quarterly limits of around 5%.

The largest listed BDC shows the same pattern in miniature. Ares Capital reported third-quarter 2026 total investment income of $763 million and core earnings of $0.46 per share against a $0.48 dividend, covering about 96% of the payout. Non-accruals, loans where the borrower has fallen behind and the lender has stopped booking interest, rose to 2.4% of the portfolio at amortized cost at September 30, 2026, from 1.8% at the end of 2025. First-lien senior secured loans made up 58% of its portfolio. The dividend has been stable or rising for 69 consecutive quarters. The direction of the troubled-loan line is the number to watch.

The giants are not retreating

None of this has stopped the largest managers from deploying. Ares Management ended the third quarter with $691 billion of assets under management and $416 billion of fee-paying assets, up 16% and 13% year over year. Its credit funds closed approximately $6.3 billion in new U.S. direct lending commitments across 55 transactions in the quarter, and about $43.4 billion across 314 transactions over the prior twelve months. In October, Ares held a first close of more than $8 billion in equity commitments for its latest U.S. senior direct lending institutional fund, and signed an agreement to purchase a $1.1 billion direct lending portfolio from an institutional investor. Ares Capital agreed on October 26 to acquire MidCap Financial Investment Corporation in a $2.6 billion cash-and-stock deal.

Apollo has taken a different tack: transparency as a product. On October 1, the firm began providing daily pricing to investors across its $850 billion credit business, extending a program that began with investment-grade fixed income replacement products on July 1 to direct lending, asset-backed finance, multi-credit, and opportunistic credit vehicles. The goal, stated publicly, was daily mark-to-market valuations on all credit assets before the fourth quarter of 2026. Apollo and Athene pre-released a preliminary alternative net investment income estimate of roughly $375 million for the third quarter.

Blackstone, meanwhile, is financing the other side of the AI boom: its credit funds were reported to be investing $9 billion in junior debt backed by Anthropic’s five-year chip lease payments, half of a planned $18 billion junior financing, at a double-digit return. The firm was expected to report third-quarter results with total assets under management near $1.38 trillion.

The counterargument: the market is functioning

The bull case is not empty. Ares’ deployment, Apollo’s pricing initiative, and the MidCap acquisition all signal that the largest players see opportunity, not contagion. BCRED’s loan book has held up on the measures that matter to its managers: interest coverage ratios in the portfolio were 45% higher in the second quarter of 2026 than in the first quarter of 2024, and September produced a reported 1.6% monthly return after the February loss. The defaults, on the Moody’s reading, are concentrated among the smallest borrowers, and the largest managers lend to the largest companies. A 6.3% default rate in a market that lends at floating rates through a hiking cycle and a tariff shock is, on this view, a normal cyclical outcome, not a structural failure.

There is also a fair criticism of the Fitch number itself. A trailing-twelve-month default rate is a lagging indicator by construction, and it rises mechanically as the weakest 2025 vintages season. The question is not whether the rate is high. It is whether the next twelve months look worse, and on that the data is genuinely mixed: new issuance continues, spreads on quality credits remain tight, and the largest managers are buying portfolios rather than selling them.

But the bull case has a hole it does not address. Apollo’s daily pricing is not a market-clearing price. It is based on Apollo’s internal pricing methodology, benchmarked to public market data. In a stressed market, estimates can flatter. And the PIK-and-extend pattern that Moody’s documented is, by definition, a way of postponing the moment when a default is counted. A market that avoids defaults by amending them away is not a market with a 4.0% default rate. It is a market with a 5.8% rate and a 1.8-point definitional subsidy.

Underwrite the amendment, not the headline

The practical lesson is about data, not doom. The firms that will navigate the next two years are not necessarily the ones with the lowest defaults today. They are the ones that track amendments, extensions, and payment-in-kind toggles as credit events in their own books, with the same discipline they apply to missed payments. A PIK toggle is information. A maturity extension is information. A lender that records neither is underwriting on the headline rate, and the headline rate is the one number in private credit that no two reputable sources agree on.

That is why the transparency moves matter more than the deployment numbers. Daily pricing, however imperfect, creates a record that can be audited. Amendment-level data creates a record that can be priced. The market’s next phase will be won by the firms that treat documentation as data, because the 6.3% number is not really a verdict on private credit. It is a verdict on how the market counts, and the counting is about to become the business.
