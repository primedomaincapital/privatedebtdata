Private Debt Data Gaps: What LPs Can’t See (and Why It Matters)

MSCI found private credit managers withholding borrower-level data from LPs. What investors can’t see, why it matters in 2026, and how to demand better.

By Prime Domain Capital · October 2026

On March 11, 2026, MSCI published a blog post with an uncomfortable title: “What’s Really Inside Your Private-Credit Fund?” The finding was stark. Three senior direct-lending funds, modeled using their actual holdings data, showed stand-alone risk varying by more than a factor of three. Same strategy label. Triple the risk.

The reason LPs could not see that dispersion in advance is the industry’s open secret: a sizeable share of private credit managers do not disclose borrower-level financials — no EBITDA, no leverage ratios, no coverage metrics. They provide little information on the quality of underlying collateral and only limited visibility into covenant compliance or asset-level performance. In MSCI’s words, “investors are flying blind at precisely the time when vigilance matters most.”

“Investors are flying blind at precisely the time when vigilance matters most.”MSCI, March 2026

What managers don’t disclose

Walk through a typical quarterly report from a private credit fund and you will find polished performance attribution, vintage-year IRRs, and a sector breakdown in pleasing pie-chart form. What you often will not find is the loan book itself: which borrowers, at what leverage, with what covenants, secured by what.

Without granular borrower data, LPs cannot independently assess credit quality, concentration risk, or emerging stress within their own portfolios — the foundational inputs of risk management. A strategy label like “senior direct lending” turns out to conceal enormous variation in what funds actually hold. The MSCI analysis proved the point quantitatively: funds wearing the same label carried fundamentally different sources of risk, invisible to anyone reading only the marketing materials.

This is not a hypothetical concern. The CFA Institute’s 2026 research on the market found that across 53 business development companies whose loan schedules it parsed, spreads ran from 321 to 728 basis points — a dispersion that reflects real differences in credit risk hiding behind similar headline yields. In 2025, the last full origination year, median new-loan spreads across all ten sectors sat inside a tight 50-basis-point band of 475 to 525. The values on seasoned loans tell a different story. The case for examining sector allocation deliberately, rather than accepting it as a byproduct of deal flow, has rarely been stronger.

The only public window into the market

Here is the irony: the fastest-growing corner of private credit is also the least visible, and the only reliable public data comes from a regulatory accident. Business development companies — the main vehicle for retail private credit — must report every loan they hold, and its value, in quarterly SEC filings.

Among the 72 BDCs that consistently file on the SEC’s EDGAR system, non-traded net assets grew from $32 billion in early 2023 to $116 billion by the end of 2025. Listed BDCs grew from $42 billion to $55 billion over the same period. Those loan-level filings are the only public view of how a portfolio is actually built. When investors doubt a listed BDC’s loan values or sector bets, its shares fall below net asset value for all to see. A non-traded BDC’s NAV follows a valuation policy under board oversight — but is never tested by trading.

That is the state of transparency in a $2 trillion-plus market: one mandated filing regime, covering a fraction of the assets, serving as the industry’s only window. For the majority of funds holding the loans, there is limited data at all.

Why 2026 is the year this stops being acceptable

Three forces are converging to make opacity untenable.

First, the cycle. The IMF’s 2026 Global Financial Stability Report is blunt: private credit “remains a relatively opaque sector,” and “low information transparency and limited available data make it difficult to swiftly identify vulnerabilities or potential risks to financial stability.” The sector has never experienced a severe downturn at its current size. Payment defaults are rising from a low base. Vigilance matters most exactly when visibility is worst.

Second, the investor base. As retail capital floods in — Wellington estimates US retail allocations could reach $2.4 trillion by 2030 — the tolerance for “trust us” reporting will shrink. Regulators are already circling: the IMF recommends cross-border cooperation to bridge data gaps and closer monitoring of liquidity and conduct risks, particularly in retail-facing funds.

Third, the tools now exist. MSCI’s partnership with Moody’s demonstrates how standardized data can deliver transparency without sacrificing confidentiality: structured borrower-level reporting feeding private-credit risk models, implied credit ratings, probability-of-default and loss-given-default modeling, and genuine stress testing under macroeconomic shocks. The technology is not the constraint. Willingness is.

LPs have more leverage here than they use. A manager’s approach to reporting — standardized, structured, comprehensive data delivered without a fight — should influence investment decisions the same way underwriting track records do. It opens the right conversations during due diligence about how seriously a GP takes risk management. Where managers fall short, LPs are well positioned to push for improvement. Admittedly, even the best reporting cannot expose deliberate concealment. But consistent borrower-level data turns raw figures into decision-ready insight, and moves oversight from reactive to proactive — anticipating trouble and modeling it before it strikes.

The asset class grew up on relationships and reputation. At $2 trillion and counting, it needs plumbing.
