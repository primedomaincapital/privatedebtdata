Private credit went from a backwater of leveraged finance to a market the IMF sizes above $2 trillion. The figures below come from public official and research sources, not from any fund marketing deck.

In the spring of 2024, an investment committee could look at two pitch decks and see two different private credit markets. One said $1.5 trillion. The other said $3.5 trillion. Both were presented as fact, and the gap between them was not dishonesty so much as definition: some counts include only drawn loans, others add undrawn commitments, and some sweep in adjacent strategies that others leave out.

The IMF's April 2024 Global Financial Stability Report chapter on private credit cut through that fog with a deliberately plain figure: more than $2 trillion globally, with about three-quarters of it in the United States. Two years later the IMF was still using roughly $2 trillion for the direct lending universe, a sign that the market's growth had slowed from its breakneck pace rather than a sign that anyone had settled the counting argument for good.

What the numbers do agree on is the shape of the risk. Borrowers are smaller and more indebted than public-market borrowers, valuations arrive infrequently, and leverage stacks up in layers across borrowers, funds, and investors. The counterweight is real too: yields that public credit cannot match, and a long record, in Cliffwater's index data, of income doing most of the work.

Reading the numbers

Treat the $2 trillion figure as an anchor, not a census. The IMF's own briefings round it, and the difference between $1.7 trillion including undrawn commitments in the April 2024 report's detail and the headline $2 trillion-plus shows how much rides on what gets counted.

The yield figures deserve the same caution in the other direction. An 8% to 12% loan yield is a gross, pre-leverage, pre-fee number on a portfolio of loans that do not trade. Cliffwater's 7.23% net-of-fee index return over 2013–2024 is the more honest comparison point, and even it covers a period with no full default cycle at today's scale — a point the IMF itself makes when it sketches adverse scenarios with default rates of 4% to 6%.

The semiliquid slice, about $300 billion, is the number to watch. It is where lock-up logic meets redemption rights, and the IMF flags it as the channel through which a credit problem could become a liquidity problem.

Methodology

Included: public official estimates (IMF), published index returns (Cliffwater), and published yield ranges (Morningstar), each attributed to its source. Excluded: paywalled datasets (including Preqin's underlying data, used only through a public summary of its forecast), fund-level performance claims, and any figure that could not be traced to a public page found during this session. Figures were chosen for breadth across size, yield, and risk rather than for agreement with each other.

Disclaimer. Public figures change as new reports are published, and estimates from different firms are built on different definitions, so a single headline total should never be read as a precise count. Where sources disagree, both figures are shown and the difference is explained rather than averaged away.
